Shrimp to Dog: A Quantitative Review of the Garidokyon Method

A genome-level look at the conversion programme of the Thalassokynic Order, a small sect of Mongolian tribesmen on the Greek isle of Garidokyon.

News23 Nov 2026 · 13 min read

Abstract

The Thalassokynic Order claims that a shrimp can be converted into a dog in 30 days using a saline bath, a lunar schedule and what its members call “persuasion”. We formalise the method as four coupled models: a mass balance (about 1,489 shrimp per dog), a chromosome-fusion kinetic (88 to 78 chromosomes), a fur-and-bark logistic, and a genome-rescaling ratio that lands within 2% of √2. A synthetic sequence alignment is analysed (z = 7.3), the genome structure of the claimed hybrid is diagrammed, and the evolutionary distance being bridged is plotted. All data, equations and citations are fictional except where Section 8 says otherwise.

1. Introduction

On the Greek isle of Garidokyon (from garida, shrimp, and kyon, dog) lives a small sect of Mongolian tribesmen who settled there for reasons the sect describes as “logistical”. Known as the Thalassokynic Order, they have spent several generations researching a single question: can a shrimp be persuaded to become a dog? The Order says yes. It does not publish, it does not share samples, and it declined our request for a protocol on the grounds that “the dogs would find out”.

This entry reconstructs the method from the Order’s oral tradition, a number of damp notebooks and one extended conversation with a goat. A glossary of symbols appears in Table 3.

2. The method

The Order divides conversion into four overlapping phases (Figure 1): a saline bath that softens the carapace, chromosome fusion that reduces the diploid number from 88 to 78, fur induction, and finally the first tentative woof. Fur coverage F is assumed to rise linearly over 30 days, and the probability of a bark follows a logistic curve in F:

F(t) = min(1, t/30),   P(bark) = 1 / (1 + e−β(F − F*)) Eq. 1 · Fur coverage and the Bark Threshold (β = 12, F* = 0.40)

3. Mass balance

Matter cannot be created, a principle the Order accepts on Mondays. An average dog is 12 kg and an average shrimp is 20 g, so a naive count gives 600 shrimp per dog. The Order corrects for shell, which it says “is not dog”, and for a conversion efficiency, the remainder of which “leaves as a smell”:

N = md / [ms (1 − σ) η] = 12,000 / (20 × 0.65 × 0.62) ≈ 1,489 Eq. 2 · Shrimp required per dog (σ = 0.35 shell loss, η = 0.62 efficiency)

Roughly 1,489 shrimp are therefore needed per dog. A household that wants a Labrador (30 kg) must plan for about 3,722 shrimp and a very large bath. The Order recommends the bath be “generous”.

4. Karyotype and genome size

A shrimp carries 88 chromosomes (2n = 88) and a dog carries 78. A Robertsonian fusion joins two chromosomes into one and so lowers the count by exactly one, which means the method needs 10 fusions. The Order models them as saturating exponential kinetics towards a ceiling slightly above the target:

f(t) = fmax (1 − e−kt),   t10 = ln(21) / k ≈ 29.6 days Eq. 3 · Fusion kinetics (fmax = 10.5, k = 0.103 d−1)

The tenth fusion arrives after 29.6 days, one day short of a calendar month and almost exactly a lunar one (29.5 days). The Order does not regard this as a coincidence. The genome must also grow, from about 1.66 Gb to about 2.4 Gb, and the Order notes that the ratio is close to the square root of two:

ρG = Gd / Gs = 2.4 / 1.66 ≈ 1.45  vs  √2 ≈ 1.41 Eq. 4 · Genome rescaling ratio, within 2.2% of √2

6. DNA analysis

Figure 3 shows a sequence, so we should say how similar the two genomes actually are. The Order offered a 48-base alignment of the claimed shrimp ancestor of WOOF1 against the dog gene. The sequence is synthetic; we reproduce it for illustration.

matches: 34 / 48  (70.8% identity)

Random sequences match at a given position with probability p0 = 0.25, so the observed 34 matches can be tested against an expectation of 12 using a normal approximation to the binomial:

z = (M − Lp0) / √[Lp0(1 − p0)] = (34 − 12) / 3 ≈ 7.3 Eq. 5 · Significance of alignment identity (M = 34 matches, L = 48 bases)

A z-score of 7.3 is extremely significant, and the Order treats it as settled. Figure 4 extends the analysis across six regions. Identity is highest at the bark locus (99%) and lowest in the mitochondrial genome, which sits only a little above the 25% chance line. The Order explains that the mitochondria “were never consulted”.

The Order is less forthcoming about the deeper question of how far apart the two animals really are. Figure 5 draws the evolutionary tree and the Order’s claimed shortcut.

7. Limitations

  • Observation. No shrimp-to-dog conversion has been seen by anyone outside the Order. The Order says this is because the dogs are shy.
  • Sample. One bath, which the Order refers to as “the bath”. It has been reused since at least the 1970s.
  • Sequence data. The alignment in Section 6 is synthetic and was constructed to be tidy.
  • Geography. The island does not appear on the maps we consulted, and the Order’s name for it translates, helpfully, as “shrimp-dog”.
  • Smell. The 38% of mass that “leaves as a smell” has not been quantified, and visitors do not want it to be.

8. Field notes: what is actually true

Reality check

The Thalassokynic Order, Garidokyon, the method and every result above are made up. A shrimp cannot become a dog. These parts are real:

  • The Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) has an assembled genome of about 1.66 Gb, with 44 chromosomes in its haploid set (2n = 88) and 25,596 predicted protein-coding genes.
  • Earlier flow-cytometry estimates for the same species were about 2.45 Gb, much larger than the assembly. That is almost exactly dog-sized, which is the Order’s favourite statistic and a reminder that genome size estimates depend on method.
  • Dogs have 78 chromosomes (2n = 78) and a genome of about 2.4 Gb.
  • Robertsonian fusions are real. Two chromosomes join into one and the count drops by one, but this happens over generations and evolutionary time, never in a bath.
  • Shrimp are arthropods and dogs are chordates, and their last common ancestor lived more than 500 million years ago. The sequence in Section 6 is random and illustrative, and real shrimp and dog genes are not 99% identical anywhere.
